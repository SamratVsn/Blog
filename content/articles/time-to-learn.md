Two years ago I decided to take my first step towards becoming a software engineer. I was in Grade 11, and one chapter in my Computer Science textbook “Programming with C” made me wonder: **Why not learn it on my own?**

I started learning, but much more slowly than I expected. What many people could probably learn in a month took me several months. It wasn’t because I lacked interest or motivation. It was because there was always something standing between me and the time I thought I needed to learn.

Every time one responsibility ended, another replaced it. School exams became festivals, festivals became projects, projects became entrance preparation. My schedule changed, but the “perfect time” never arrived.

Since then, I’ve stopped waiting for the perfect time and simply started making the most of the time I have.

And this isn’t just about learning programming.

Whether it’s learning a language, reading many books, starting a business, going to the gym, or just learning a new skill, we often convince ourselves that we’ll begin once we feel like we have “**time**”. We imagine a day where responsibilities disappear and uninterrupted time magically appears,

It rarely does.

> **“Time is what we want most, but what we use worst.”**  
>  — William Penn

We often wait for the “right time” or the “perfect moment” to learn a new skill. The truth is, that perfect time rarely arrives on its own. If you spend your days waiting, you’ll never begin. Instead of waiting for time, you have to make time.

**The moment you decide to learn something new is the best time to start**. It doesn’t matter if you can only spare a few minutes a day. What matters is that you begin. By managing our time wisely, we can balance our studies, exercise, household responsibilities, personal interests, and even our free time. Time management isn’t about being busy every second: **It’s about giving your time to what truly matters**.

Many of us spend hours on things that add little value while neglecting the activities that help us grow. Learning to distinguish between what is important and what is merely distracting allows us to use our time more effectively. No matter how busy your schedule is, most people can set aside 30 minutes to an hour each day to learn something new.

Learning is built on consistency. Spending one hour every day is far more effective than studying for seven hours in a single day and then doing nothing for the rest of the week. Small, consistent efforts compound over time, turning knowledge into skill and skill into mastery.

> **“Do not wait; the time will never be ‘just right.’ Start where you stand.”**  
>  — Napoleon Hill

Time isn’t what we’re waiting for. It’s something we choose to use wisely. The perfect moment rarely arrives on its own, but progress begins the moment you decide to start. Every small step you take today brings you closer to the person you want to become.
